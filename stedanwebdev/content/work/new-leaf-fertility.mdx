## The situation

New Leaf connects intended parents with fertility clinics in Ghana — surrogacy primarily, alongside IVF, egg donation and egg freezing. Most countries restrict or ban commercial surrogacy. Ghana permits it, at a fraction of UK or US cost.

So the audience is almost entirely international, and the sale is almost entirely trust. Someone in London or Berlin has to believe an organisation 5,000 miles away is legitimate enough to begin a medical process involving a child, before meeting anyone or seeing anything.

That has a design consequence most brand work doesn't face. There is no flagship surface. A couple might first encounter New Leaf through a flyer at a fertility event, a banner behind a stage, a business card handed over after a conversation, or a treatment guide found at 2am. Every one of those has to carry the same weight, because any one of them might be the only one.

When I joined there was a brand document, and a three-page website: home, about, contact. Nothing to read, nothing to verify, nobody to see, and no applied assets of any kind.

## My role

Marketing Associate, covering brand, digital assets and the website, on a founding team of four alongside the two co-founders and the operations manager.

The brand guidelines existed before me. What didn't exist was anything made from them — no templates, no collateral, no applied system. That's what I built, and then extended as the organisation found new places it needed to show up.

I did not write the articles. Copy came from the team and, later, from clinical partners.

## Building the applied identity

The guidelines gave me a wordmark, a palette and a typeface. Everything below is what I made with them.

### Print and collateral

Business cards for the founding team, built as a template so new joiners don't need a designer. Flyers, front and back — the front doing one job (what this is, one QR code to book), the back doing the harder one: why Ghana, what's included, who handles what.

The back of that flyer is the piece I'd defend most. Someone considering surrogacy abroad has a specific list of fears — is it legal, is it safe, who looks after me there. It answers them in that order, in two columns, on a page someone can fold into a bag.

### Event assets

Roll-up banners, designed in three variants so a stand doesn't read as one repeated message. They were made for a live fertility event and have been reused several times since, which is the real test of a template — whether it survives the occasion it was made for.

The variants split by job: one leads with possibility, one with the brand line, one with the practical ask and a QR code. A stand needs all three registers, not three copies of one.

### Decks and ongoing assets

Presentation templates, social graphics, video edits. The unglamorous half of brand work, and the half that decides whether an identity holds together six months after the guidelines are written.

## Building the platform

### From brochure to content library

I built out roughly 20 CMS collections so the site could hold depth instead of pitching: articles, treatment guides, quick facts, FAQs, team members, authors, categories, careers, events.

Live today: **7 articles, 4 treatment guides, 22 treatment quick facts, 39 FAQs, 8 team members.**

### Human copy inside a clinical approval gate

Early articles were flat, and nothing had medical sign-off. Those two problems pull against each other. Conversational copy is what an anxious person actually absorbs; clinical accuracy is what makes it safe to publish. The usual resolution is to go stiff and defensive, which is why most fertility content reads like a liability waiver.

Once we partnered with a clinic, I set up a workflow where treatment guides go to a doctor for approval before publishing, and kept the tone readable inside that gate. The process still runs now that the team has grown.

### One home for the FAQs

The FAQs had grown to 39 and were scattered — some on programme pages, some on guides, some in the footer. Nobody could tell you what the organisation's answer to a question actually was, because there were several.

I consolidated them into a single categorised page — Getting Started, Surrogacy, Surrogate Welfare, Legal and Documentation, Costs and Payments, Egg Freezing — and left the contextual ones pulling from the same source rather than living separately.

The usability gain is real, but the operational one matters more: there is now one place where an answer lives, and changing it changes it everywhere.

### Put faces on the site

A team page, with eight people on it — co-founders, operations, medical governance, marketing. In a cross-border business where the underlying fear is *is this real*, showing who is actually there does more work than any paragraph of copy.

### Lead with process, hold back price

Treatment guides open with process and facts. There is no pricing anywhere on the site — deliberate, because we don't yet have standard pricing we'd stand behind publicly.

I hold this as an open question rather than a settled decision. Cost is a large part of why an international couple is looking at Ghana at all, and a page with no price signal may be filtering out people who would have qualified.

### Build the index, not the template

I set out to build a full events template in the CMS. Every event needed a different structure, and no standard shape existed that didn't require fresh setup each time. So I built the events index — filterable by location and status — and moved event detail to Luma rather than forcing a template nobody could maintain.

The index does the discovery job on our own site; Luma does the registration job it's already good at. The right call, reached later than it should have been.

## Then I looked at the numbers

Five and a half months of GA4, April to September 2026:

| | |
|---|---|
| Sessions | 7,063 |
| Active users | 5,392 |
| Engaged sessions | 1,839 (26%) |
| Avg. session duration | 1m 28s |
| Key events configured | **0** |
| Email captures (Brevo) | **12** |

And the landing pages:

| Landing page | Sessions | Engagement rate |
|---|---|---|
| `/book-a-consultation` | **4,893** | **16.19%** |
| `/` | 1,438 | 47.44% |
| `/careers` | 268 | 60% |
| `/about-us` | 113 | 56.62% |
| `/programs` | 47 | 68.09% |

**69% of all traffic lands directly on the booking page, and that page has the worst engagement rate on the site.** Not one article or treatment guide appears in the top six.

Paid search was routing almost everything straight to the booking form, skipping the library entirely. The pages that engage people — home at 47%, careers at 60%, programs at 68% — were getting a fraction of the traffic.

### And the measurement was hiding it

The primary Google Ads conversion, *Book appointment*, fires on **page load** of the booking page and counts every occurrence. Between 6 February and 19 September 2026 it recorded **3,193 conversions**.

Those are page loads. The action that represents an actual lead — the Tally form submission — recorded **zero**, meaning the tag never fires. GA4 has no key events configured at all.

So the number that looked like performance was measuring the dump, and the number that mattered was never captured. 12 email addresses from 7,063 sessions is the real conversion picture: **0.17%**.

## What I'd change

1. **Fix the tracking first.** Tally form submission as the primary conversion, page-load demoted to secondary, key events configured in GA4. Nothing else can be judged until this is right.
2. **Re-route paid traffic to the guides, not the form.** Someone researching surrogacy abroad is not ready to book on first click. Send them to the treatment guide matching the ad, then offer the consultation.
3. **Give the library an entry point.** Seven articles nobody lands on is a distribution problem, not a content problem.
4. **Test a price signal.** Not a price list — a range, or a comparison. The current zero-signal approach is untested either way.

## What I found on the way

While pulling screenshots for this write-up I checked the colour contrast on the treatment guide body sections. White text was sitting on a mid-orange band that measured well below the 4.5:1 WCAG AA minimum for body copy — on the longest reading passage on the page, for an audience that skews anxious and often older.

Changing the band to a deep olive brought it above 7:1, which clears AA comfortably. The layout didn't need to change at all.

The design decision was sound; the colour value wasn't. Nobody catches that by looking.

## What I'd take from it

Two years of this taught me one thing more than anything else: **consistency across surfaces is a systems problem, not a taste problem.**

The reason the flyer, the banner, the card and the site look like one organisation isn't that I made good individual choices. It's that each one was built as a template with rules, so the next person making one doesn't have to have taste — they have to follow the system.

The second lesson is less comfortable. I spent most of my time building capability, and it holds up. But the funnel was routing around it, and I didn't find that until I stopped looking at totals and started looking at landing-page engagement. Building the thing and getting people to it are two separate jobs, and the second one is easier to skip because the first one feels like progress.
