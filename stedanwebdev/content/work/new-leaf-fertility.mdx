## The situation

New Leaf connects international intended parents with trusted clinics and compassionate carriers in Ghana. The work is surrogacy, primarily, alongside IVF, egg donation and egg freezing, and the audience is global — individuals and couples around the world, with a particular reach into the diaspora.

That makes trust the entire proposition. Someone has to believe an organisation operating thousands of miles away is legitimate enough to begin a medical process involving a child, often before meeting anyone in person.

When I joined in January 2026, New Leaf had two things. A complete brand book — purpose, voice, taglines, logo, palette, typography, photography direction — written by the co-founder. And a three-page website: home, about, contact.

What it did not have was anything made from the brand book. No templates, no collateral, no content, no applied system of any kind. The brand was fully specified and almost entirely unbuilt.

That gap was the job.

## What I built

### The content platform

I took the site from three pages to a working library, built on roughly 20 CMS collections: articles, treatment guides, quick facts, FAQs, team members, authors, categories, careers, events.

Live today: **7 articles, 4 treatment guides, 22 treatment quick facts, 39 FAQs, 8 team members.**

The point of the structure is that nobody needs me to publish. Content, images and copy are all editable by the team.

### Print and event assets

Business cards, built as a template so a new joiner doesn't need a designer. A two-sided flyer — the front doing one job, the back answering the questions people actually arrive with. Roll-up banners in three variants, made for a live fertility event and reused several times since.

Plus the ongoing work: presentation templates, social graphics, video edits. The unglamorous half, and the half that decides whether a brand book survives contact with a real organisation.

## Three decisions

### Human copy inside a clinical approval gate

Early articles were flat, and nothing carried medical sign-off. Those two problems pull against each other — conversational copy is what an anxious person absorbs, clinical accuracy is what makes it safe to publish. The usual resolution is to go stiff and defensive, which is why most fertility content reads like a liability waiver.

Once we partnered with a clinic, I set up a workflow where treatment guides go to a doctor for approval before publishing, and kept the tone readable inside that gate. It still runs now the team has grown.

### One home for the answers

The FAQs had grown past thirty and were scattered across programme pages, guides and the footer. Nobody could say what the organisation's answer to a question was, because there were several versions of it.

I consolidated them into one categorised page, with contextual FAQs elsewhere pulling from the same source. The usability gain is real. The operational one matters more: there's now one place an answer lives, and changing it changes it everywhere.

### Build the index, not the template

I set out to build a full events template in the CMS, and found every event needed a different structure. No standard shape existed that didn't require fresh setup each time.

So I built the events index — filterable by location and status — and moved event detail to Luma. The index does discovery on our own site; Luma does registration, which it's already good at. That was the right call, reached later than it should have been.

## Where it got to

Between April and September 2026 the site drew **7,063 sessions from 5,392 users**, with traffic arriving from the UK, Ghana, the United States and Côte d'Ivoire — from a standing start, on a site that had been three pages a few months earlier.

The library, the approval workflow and the asset templates are all still in use, and the team publishes without me.

## What I'd do differently

**I'd have questioned the price decision earlier.** There's no pricing anywhere on the site, deliberately, because we don't yet have standard pricing we'd stand behind publicly. But cost is part of why an international couple looks at Ghana at all, and a page with no price signal may be filtering out people who would have qualified. I still think the caution was right; I should have proposed a test sooner.

**I'd have reached the Luma decision faster.** I spent time building toward a template the content didn't support. The signal was there early — every event needing its own structure — and I kept going anyway.

**I'd have checked applied colour against the palette sooner.** The treatment guides ran white body text on Terracotta, one of the brand accents. It measures 2.98:1, below the 4.5:1 minimum for body copy — on the longest reading passage on the site, for an audience that skews anxious and often older. Moving it to Deep Olive, another palette colour, brings it to 8.2:1. The palette was never wrong. The role it was doing was.
